# CalBallot audience pitches

> Product-positioning draft, 2026-08-27. These pitches describe CalBallot's
> intended value to repeat users. Public copy should continue to distinguish
> broad historical coverage from the narrower set of counties captured for a
> current election. CalBallot is not yet an address-level “your ballot” tool.

## Local and statewide journalists

### Follow the measure—not just the election-day headline

California ballot measures are reported in fragments. Qualification notices
appear on government websites, campaign money lives in disclosure systems,
passage thresholds vary by instrument, and historical results are scattered
across decades of state and county records. Reporting a single measure can
require assembling information from several sources before the substantive work
even begins.

CalBallot brings that material into one searchable research environment.

Journalists can use CalBallot to quickly establish the basic facts of a measure:
its jurisdiction, election year, legal passage threshold, official source,
outcome, vote share, classification, and—where available—campaign-finance
activity. Historical search makes it possible to move beyond the immediate
contest and ask better questions: How often have similar measures appeared? Is
this county unusually active? Have comparable bonds or taxes generally passed?
How often have measures won a majority but failed because they required 55% or
two-thirds?

For statewide propositions, CalBallot connects election results with matched
campaign-finance records. That helps reporters identify the scale and balance of
spending without treating money as a prediction or a complete explanation of
voter behavior. Its Rules analysis also makes a frequently misunderstood point
visible: a measure can receive more yes votes than no votes and still fail under
a supermajority requirement.

CalBallot is particularly useful for local reporting, where ballot measures
receive less sustained coverage and official material is often difficult to
find. Its emerging registrar pipeline captures current county records with
source attribution and stable measure identities. The long-term goal is to
follow a measure from initial publication through official documents, campaign
activity, election results, and historical comparison.

CalBallot does not tell readers how to vote, and it does not replace
primary-source reporting. It helps reporters find those sources, verify
foundational facts, spot patterns worth investigating, and avoid rebuilding the
same research spreadsheet for every election.

**Use CalBallot as the starting desk for ballot-measure reporting: establish the
record quickly, find the revealing comparison, and follow the evidence back to
its source.**

## Academic and policy researchers

### A structured view of California's ballot-measure history

California's initiative and local-measure systems offer an extraordinary record
of direct democracy—but studying that record is difficult. Relevant data come
from state archives, county election offices, academic datasets,
campaign-finance disclosures, and changing administrative websites. Differences
in naming, jurisdiction, election year, measure type, vote threshold, and source
quality make longitudinal analysis especially costly.

CalBallot provides a consolidated research layer over this fragmented record.

The database contains more than 12,000 active statewide and local measure
records, including statewide history extending back to 1911 and extensive local
coverage across California's counties. Measures can be examined by year,
geography, topic, instrument, passage threshold, outcome, and vote share. The
public data bundle is structured and machine-readable, while the site provides
immediate descriptive exploration without requiring a custom research
environment.

CalBallot is well suited to questions about institutional design and electoral
behavior:

- How do passage rates differ between simple-majority, 55%, and two-thirds
  contests?
- Which measure types dominate local ballots?
- How has ballot activity changed across election cycles?
- Where do majority-supported measures fail because of higher legal thresholds?
- How do finance patterns vary across matched statewide campaigns?
- How do classifications, outcomes, and coverage differ across source datasets?

The project treats provenance and uncertainty as part of the data rather than
inconvenient footnotes. Known source limitations are documented, including
unreliable derived thresholds in a small number of historical records.
Registrar-sourced thresholds are distinguished from aggregator-derived fields.
The ingestion architecture preserves stable identities and immutable source
observations so future work can examine how election information changes before
voting occurs—not merely the final published result.

CalBallot is not presented as a perfectly complete or analysis-ready causal
dataset. Coverage varies across periods and sources, classifications are uneven,
and observed relationships should not be mistaken for causal effects. Its value
is a transparent, inspectable foundation from which researchers can define
samples, audit assumptions, identify anomalies, and construct more rigorous
studies.

**CalBallot offers researchers a rare combination: broad historical scope,
structured local and statewide records, explicit data-quality limits, and a
pathway from derived observations back to public sources.**

## Civic-information organizations

### Build voter information on a shared, verifiable foundation

Voters often encounter ballot measures as isolated blocks of legal language,
campaign advertising, or recommendations from organizations they already agree
with. The underlying facts—what jurisdiction placed the measure on the ballot,
what vote it needs, where the official text lives, and how it relates to earlier
measures—are much harder to assemble.

Civic-information organizations repeatedly perform this foundational work.
CalBallot can make that work reusable.

CalBallot organizes California ballot measures into a common, searchable
structure spanning statewide propositions and thousands of local contests. Each
record is designed to connect basic identity, geography, measure type, passage
rule, outcome, historical context, and available source links. Rather than
offering endorsements, CalBallot gives users the factual scaffolding needed to
understand what they are being asked to decide.

That makes it useful across several civic-information programs:

- Voter-guide research and fact checking
- Classroom and public-education materials
- Ballot-measure explainers
- Election workshops and community forums
- Historical comparisons and data visualizations
- Links from advocacy-neutral voter resources to official records

The project's distinction between deterministic facts and editorial
interpretation is especially important. A vote threshold or official document
link can be published directly when its provenance is clear. A summary, argument,
or fiscal claim requires stronger evidence and should not be inferred merely
because a document exists. CalBallot's architecture is designed around that
discipline.

The site is static, public, and inexpensive to operate, which supports long-term
availability without advertising or a costly backend. Its structured public data
can also serve organizations that want to conduct their own analysis or create
audience-specific presentations while retaining links to the underlying record.

CalBallot is not yet an address-level ballot lookup, and current local-election
coverage must always be described county by county. It should complement—not
impersonate—official election tools. Its strongest role is as shared civic
infrastructure beneath guides, lessons, reporting, and public conversations.

**Instead of every organization rebuilding the same fragmented measure history,
CalBallot offers a common factual layer: public, searchable, source-conscious,
and neutral about how anyone should vote.**

## Government, campaign, and public-affairs professionals

### Understand the ballot-measure landscape before making strategic claims

Ballot-measure work often begins with deceptively simple questions: Has this
jurisdiction tried something similar? What threshold applies? How have comparable
instruments performed? Is a majority sufficient? How crowded is the ballot? What
historical comparison is actually defensible?

Answering those questions reliably can require searching across county records,
statewide archives, historical datasets, and campaign-finance disclosures.
CalBallot reduces that initial research burden by organizing California's
ballot-measure record in one place.

Professionals can use CalBallot to examine measures by jurisdiction, year, type,
topic, passage threshold, outcome, and vote share. The historical database makes
it easier to distinguish between genuinely comparable measures and superficial
analogies. A school bond requiring 55%, for example, should not be evaluated as
though it were an ordinary simple-majority question. A special tax requiring
two-thirds exists in a different institutional environment from a general tax—even
if both concern revenue.

For statewide campaigns and public-affairs work, CalBallot also provides matched
campaign-finance context. Users can examine support and opposition activity
alongside the election result while avoiding the common mistake of treating the
better-funded side as automatically favored or victorious.

Government users may find particular value in cross-jurisdiction comparisons and
data-quality checks. CalBallot can help identify naming inconsistencies,
threshold anomalies, repeated measure types, and gaps between official county
records and historical aggregators. Its registrar architecture is designed to
preserve source observations over time, supporting future analysis of when
measures and official documents were published or revised.

CalBallot is not a forecasting model, persuasion platform, voter file, or
substitute for legal review. Historical pass rates do not establish the prospects
of a new proposal, and the database should not be used to flatten meaningful
differences in language, electorate, timing, or jurisdiction. Its value comes
earlier in the process: establishing the factual landscape and testing whether a
strategic narrative survives contact with the record.

**CalBallot gives government and public-affairs professionals a faster, more
disciplined way to research California ballot measures—grounded in legal rules,
historical outcomes, geography, finance, and traceable public sources.**
